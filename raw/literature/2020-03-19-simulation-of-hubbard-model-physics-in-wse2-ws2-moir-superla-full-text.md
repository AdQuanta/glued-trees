Simulation of Hubbard model physics in WSe2/WS2 moiré superlattices
Yanhao Tang1, Lizhong Li1, Tingxin Li1, Yang Xu1, Song Liu2, Katayun Barmak3, Kenji Watanabe4, Takashi Taniguchi4, Allan H. MacDonald5, Jie Shan1,6,7✉ & Kin Fai Mak1,6,7✉
The Hubbard model, formulated by physicist John Hubbard in the 1960s1, is a simple theoretical model of interacting quantum particles in a lattice. The model is thought to capture the essential physics of high-temperature superconductors, magnetic insulators and other complex quantum many-body ground states2,3. Although the Hubbard model provides a greatly simplified representation of most real materials, it is nevertheless difficult to solve accurately except in the one-dimensional case2,3. Therefore, the physical realization of the Hubbard model in two or three dimensions, which can act as an analogue quantum simulator (that is, it can mimic the model and simulate its phase diagram and dynamics4,5), has a vital role in solving the strongcorrelation puzzle, namely, revealing the physics of a large number of strongly interacting quantum particles. Here we obtain the phase diagram of the twodimensional triangular-lattice Hubbard model by studying angle-aligned WSe2/WS2 bilayers, which form moiré superlattices6 because of the difference between the lattice constants of the two materials. We probe the charge and magnetic properties of the system by measuring the dependence of its optical response on an out-of-plane magnetic field and on the gate-tuned carrier density. At half-filling of the first hole moiré superlattice band, we observe a Mott insulating state with antiferromagnetic Curie–Weiss behaviour, as expected for a Hubbard model in the strong-interaction regime2,3,7–9. Above half-filling, our experiment suggests a possible quantum phase transition from an antiferromagnetic to a weak ferromagnetic state at filling factors near 0.6. Our results establish a new solid-state platform based on moiré superlattices that can be used to simulate problems in strong-correlation physics that are described by triangular-lattice Hubbard models.
The Hubbard model Hamiltonian has only two terms1–3, a kinetic lattice hopping term (t) and an on-site Coulomb repulsion interaction term (U). The Hubbard model on a triangular lattice is particularly rich because geometrical frustration enhances quantum fluctuations and leads to a multitude of closely competing states3,8–10 that challenge theory3. The discovery of superconductivity and correlated insulating states in twisted graphene systems has opened a new chapter in strong-electronic-correlation physics11–20. A recent theoretical analysis has shown that moiré superlattices formed by semiconductor transition metal dichacogenide (TMD) heterobilayers provide a realization of the triangular-lattice Hubbard model6. Unlike graphene systems, in which spin, valley and layer degeneracies are all present, the spin and layer degeneracies in TMD heterobilayers are lifted by the strong spin–orbit interactions6 and the layer asymmetry, respectively. When the chemical potential is within the topmost valence band, where there is large spin–orbit splitting6, holes can be mapped to the single-band Hubbard model with two valley degrees of freedom, which acts as the spin. Here we study the phase diagram of aligned WSe2/WS2 bilayers
around half-filling in the limit of U/t≫1 by performing optical spectroscopy on moiré excitons21–25, supplemented by transport measurements. Heat extraction via a phonon bath and simple gating procedures allow the Hubbard model phase diagram to be explored over a wider range of density and temperature than what is achievable in cold atoms in optical lattices5.
Moiré excitons in WSe2/WS2 heterobilayer
There is a ~4% lattice mismatch between WSe2 and WS2, which produces a triangular moiré superlattice with a periodicity close to 8 nm for an angle-aligned bilayer (Fig. 1a). Two symmetric graphene gates are fabricated above and below the bilayer to introduce carriers into the moiré superlattice (Fig. 1c). Applying equal voltages (Vg) on both gates varies the carrier density in the bilayer while keeping the vertical electric field unchanged. (See Methods for details on sample fabrication, the determination of the twist angle and the effect of the vertical electric field.) WSe2/WS2 bilayers are known to have type-II band alignment6,23 (Fig. 1b). This is fully consistent with our results for the gate-dependent reflection contrast spectrum of a misaligned bilayer (near 20° twist angle) and 60° aligned bilayer (see Methods for details on optical measurements). In the misaligned bilayer (Fig. 1d), the two most prominent features are the neutral A exciton resonance of WSe2 and WS2. For Vg between the dashed horizontal lines, both A excitons are present, indicating that the Fermi level is inside the energy gap of the bilayer. Doping into the WS2 conduction band or the WSe2 valence band suppresses the neutral A exciton and creates a charged exciton in the corresponding layer, while the neutral A exciton in the other layer remains nearly unaffected. In the aligned bilayer (Fig. 1e), multiple peaks emerge around the original A excitons when the Fermi level is inside the energy gap of the bilayer (Vg between the dashed horizontal lines). This agrees with
previous reports on the observation of moiré excitons21–23 generated by the periodic moiré potential in each layer. The moiré excitons exhibit distinct gate dependences from that of the A exciton in the misaligned bilayer. In particular, quasiperiodic modulations are observed in the reflection contrast, which are correlated with half-integer moiré band fillings, as we discuss in the following. We focus on the effect of filling the first hole moiré band of WSe2 (boxed region), the Hamiltonian of which has been shown to map to the single-band Hubbard model on a triangular lattice when interactions are taken into account6.

Figure 2b shows the reflectance peak amplitude of the lowest-energy moiré exciton as a function of filling factor. Prominent peaks on a smooth background (that decreases with n/n0) are noted at half- and full filling. These are correlated with peaks in the two-point resistance of the WSe2 layer (Fig. 2c). The resistance is dominated by the large contact resistance at low filling factors, but it is dominated by the sample resistance at half- and full filling at low temperature (see Methods for the effects of the contact resistance). The substantially stronger temperature dependence of the resistance at these filling factors, compared to that away from them (for example, at filling factor 0.8; see inset of Fig. 2c), supports an insulating state at half- and full filling. Whereas the insulating behaviour at full filling of the first moiré band is expected for an isolated band even in the absence of interactions, the insulating behaviour at half-filling is of a different origin. When U/t ≫ 1, the strong on-site repulsion suppresses double occupancy of the moiré lattice. Each lattice site (located at the maximum of the effective valence band potential energy) is occupied by one hole, giving rise to a Mott insulator with a gap energy of about U (refs. 6,27). We find that the insulating behaviour persists up to about 150 K (Fig. 2c), providing a lower-bound estimate of 10–20 meV for U. This estimate is consistent with the prediction for TMD heterobilayers6. The large moiré exciton reflection contrast in the insulating states is intriguing. It probably arises from quenched free-carrier screening of the electron–hole interactions, particularly the formation of polarons28. The neutral exciton resonance therefore partially regains oscillator strength and reflection contrast.
Magnetic interaction at half-filling
The ground and low-energy excited states of the Mott state are determined by interactions between the localized magnetic moments that survive charge localization. To gain insight into the magnetic interactions, we measure the helicity-resolved reflection contrast spectrum of the lowest-energy moiré exciton as a function of out-of-plane magnetic field H (Fig. 3a). The field is known to break the valley degeneracy, and each handedness of light couples to only one of the two valleys29. Leftand right-handed reflections acquire an energy splitting of EZ and have different amplitudes. We analyse the field dependence of EZ (which is assumed to be proportional to the spin/valley polarization) in Fig. 3b. (SeeMethods for details on the analysis of EZ and the reflection amplitude.) At half-filling, EZ increases linearly with the field at small fields and saturates to about 9 meV above about 1 T. Magnetic interaction at different fillings
Next we examine the physics of doped Mott insulators away from halffilling. Figure 4a shows the field dependence of EZ at different fillings. EZ saturates above about 1 T, indicating that full valley polarization is always achieved. The filling dependence of the EZ saturation value follows that of the magnetization saturation value, as evaluated from the localized moment of singly occupied sites, suggesting that λX is weakly dependent on the filling (Extended Data Fig. 4). The low-field slope of EZ yields the exciton g-factor, which is summarized in Fig. 4c for different fillings at 1.6K (≪t). The g-factor takes the bare-exciton g0 (g0 ≈ −4) near zero and full filling, and increases by nearly two orders of magnitude towards the middle between filling factors 0.5 and 0.7. Increasing temperature rapidly quenches the steep increase of the g-factor (Extended Data Fig.5), which suggests a divergence in the zerotemperature limit. Figure 4b compares the temperature dependence of χ−1 ∝ |g0/(g – g0)| with the Curie–Weiss law at various fillings. For all cases the data follow the Curie–Weiss law at high temperatures. The best-fit Weiss constant θ (Fig. 4c) is negative for filling factors below 0.6, suggesting antiferromagnetic interactions or Fermi pressure of the moiré band holes, but stays near or above zero for filling factors above 0.6, suggesting a weak ferromagnetic interaction (θ ≳ 0). The latter is uncertain, given the experimental uncertainty in θ. The extracted Weiss constant is correlated with the field dependence of EZ at 1.6 K in Fig. 4a. At filling factors near 0.6, where the magnetic interaction between localized moments is weak, the dependence is approximately the Brillouin function, which is typical for non-interacting localized spins. However, for the three filling factors below 0.5, the data suggest a metamagnetic-like transition around 0.5–1T, a behaviour often seen in antiferromagnetic systems. Magnetic ordering at these low filling factors is compatible with the larger |θ| and the absence of frustration. The observed divergent χ, together with the continuously vanishing θ, at filling factors near 0.6 suggests a possible quantum phase transition from an antiferromagnetic state to a weak ferromagnetic state, with a ferromagnetic transition temperature substantially lower than the temperature accessible in our study. For a continuous magnetic quantum phase transition, the exchange coupling vanishes at the quantum critical point, where χ (a measure of the magnetic correlation) diverges33. The existence of a quantum critical point near filling factor 0.6 has also been suggested by numerical simulations of the triangular-lattice Hubbard model9. Figure 4d further shows the scaling
analysis of the magnetization (g0μBμ0λXM) at different temperatures as a function of magnetic field normalized by temperature (H/T) at a filling factor of 0.61 (see Extended Data Fig. 6 for raw data). This is obtained by subtracting the bare-exciton Zeeman splitting from EZ. The temperature dependences spanning more than a decade collapse to one curve, demonstrating M ∝ H/T for small fields or the Curie–Weiss law, χ−1 ∝ T , near the quantum critical point (that is, the temperature is the only relevant energy scale). Finally, we note that the asymmetric phase diagram in Fig. 4c around half-filling is correlated with the asymmetric flat band of TMD heterobilayers, which has a van Hove singularity in the density of states at 3/4 filling6. Whether superconductivity emerges in WSe2/WS2 bilayers near the quantum critical point deserves further investigation by low-temperature transport measurements, which could also shed additional light on the nature of the ground states away from the quantum critical point.

 Methods
Sample preparation and device fabrication
WSe2 bulk crystals were synthesized using a flux method by reacting W with a Se flux. The starting materials, W powder (99.999%) and Se shot (99.999%) were mixed in an atomic ratio of 1:15 and loaded into a quartz ampule evacuated and sealed at ~10−6 torr. The ampule was placed vertically into a box furnace and heated to 1,080 °C for 24h. After a dwell time of a week at 1,080 °C, it was cooled to 300 °C at a rate of 4.5 °C h−1. Crystals were subsequently filtered from the Se flux by quartz wool and annealed at 250 °C for 48 h in a vacuum quartz ampule. WS2 bulk crystals were acquired from HQ Graphene. The dual-gate WSe2/WS2 bilayer devices were built from exfoliated van der Waals materials using a layer-by-layer dry-transfer method34. All atomically thin flakes were exfoliated from bulk crystals onto oxidized Si substrates. Monolayer WS2 and WSe2 flakes were identified by their optical contrast. The crystal orientations of WS2 and WSe2 were determined by optical second-harmonic generation (SHG) as described below. hBN flakes with a thickness of about 20 nm were used as the gate dielectrics. Devices used for optical measurements had few-layer graphite as a contact electrode, and the entire structure was assembled and released onto Si substrates with prepatterned Au electrodes in a single step. Devices for transport measurements were built in three steps. First, a bottom gate made of graphite and hBN was prepared on a Si substrate. Next, Pt electrodes were evaporated onto the bottom gate using electron beam lithography and evaporation. Finally, the rest of the device was assembled and released onto the Pt electrodes. Alignment between the WS2 and WSe2 crystals was achieved using a rotation stage with a precision better than 1°. (Because the two layers have different lattice constants and because there is no magic-angle property, the sensitivity of the device properties to the twist angle is much weaker than in graphene multilayers.) The stamp employed to pick up flakes from substrates was made of a polycarbonate layer on polypropylene-carbonate-coated polydimethylsiloxane. Vander Waals materials were released from the stamp to the substrate at about 180 °C. The polycarbonate residue on the devices was removed by dissolving it in chloroform, followed by a rinse in isopropyl alcohol. Sample transfer was performed in a nitrogen-filled glovebox for better interface quality. Extended Data Fig. 8 shows an optical micrograph of a typical device.
Angle-resolved optical SHG
Angle-resolved SHG was employed to determine the crystal orientations of the TMDs. During the measurement, samples were kept in vacuum with a pressure of less than 0.1 torr to avoid potential optical damage. The output of a Ti:sapphire oscillator (Spectra Physics, Tsunami) with a peak photon energy of 1.55eV, a repetition rate of 80MHz and a pulse duration of 100 fs was used as the excitation source. An excitation beam of less than 2 mW was focused onto the TMD flakes under normal incidence through a microscope objective with a numerical aperture of 0.6. The beam diameter on the samples was about 2μm. The second-harmonic signal was collected by the same objective in reflection geometry, and detected by a spectrometer coupled with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) camera. A shortpass filter was used in the detection path to cut off the fundamental wavelength. The excitation was linearly polarized. An achromatic halfwave plate was used to control the excitation polarization with respect to the sample orientation. The reflected second-harmonic beam was passed through the same half-wave plate and an analyser that was kept perpendicular to the polarization of the excitation light. The SHG from monolayer TMDs follows a six-fold pattern. The peak corresponds to the zigzag direction of the crystal35. In bilayers with 0° stacking the SHG is much enhanced, and in bilayers with 60° stacking it is largely cancelled. To determine the twist angle in bilayers quantitatively, we measured the angle-resolved SHG in the non-overlapping region of the bilayer for both WS2 and WSe2, determined their orientations and
extracted the twist angle assuming the strain and other distortions in the overlapping region to be negligible. For the device shown in Fig. 1, the twist angle in the bilayer was determined to be 0.4° ± 0.2° (Extended Data Fig. 9).

Effect of out-of-plane electric field
The dual-gate device structure allows independent tuning of the doping density (by symmetric gating) and the out-of-plane electric field (by antisymmetric gating) in the bilayer. However, because of the large built-in electric field in the heterobilayer, all of the reported observations are insensitive to applied electric fields accessible in this experiment (Extended Data Fig. 11).
Optical measurements
Devices were mounted in a closed-cycle cryostat (Attocube, Attodry 2100) for magneto-optical measurements down to 1.65 K. A halogen lamp was used as a white-light source for the reflection measurements. The output of the lamp was collected by a single-mode fibre, collimated by a 10× objective and then focused onto the sample by an objective with a numerical aperture of 0.8. The beam diameter on the sample was about 1 μm and the beam power was less than 1 nW. The reflected light was collected by the same objective and detected by a spectrometer coupled with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled CCD camera. To probe the lowest-energy moiré exciton in WSe2 as a function of doping density, a long-pass filter was used in the excitation path to cut off light below 700nm to avoid photo-doping effects by short-wavelength illumination. A combination of a linear polarizer and an achromatic quarter-wave plate was used to generate circularly polarized white light. The reflection contrast (R) spectrum was obtained by comparing the reflected light intensity from the sample (I′) to that of a featureless

spectrum (I) from the sample in the highly hole-doped regime or from the substrate. The reflection contrast was extracted as R≡(I′ − I)/I. The measurement sensitivity was ~0.1%. The angle-aligned TMD heterostructures can also host moiré interlayer exciton states. The optical response of interlayer moiré excitons is also sensitive to doping. Owing to its interlayer nature, optical absorption of the interlayer moiré exciton is negligible and cannot be measured. The photoluminescence of interlayer moiré excitons was measured (Extended Data Fig.12). Unlike optical absorption, the emission intensity is complicated by the interlayer exciton lifetime (which also varies with doping).
Transport measurements
The electrical measurements were carried out in a closed-cycle cryostat (Oxford, TeslatronPT) down to 1.5 K. An a.c. bias of 10–20 mV at 17 Hz was applied between the source and drain, and the source–drain current was measured with a lock-in amplifier.
Effects of contact resistance on optical and transport measurements
The large contact resistance of TMD semiconductors, particularly in the low-doping regime at low temperature, remains a challenge. However, the large contact resistance has minimal effects on the measurements when the electrodes are employed only to gate the sample (Figs. 1, 2 except Fig. 2c). The RC time constant for charging the system is estimated as <1 ms (resistance <1 GΩ and gate capacitance ~10 fF). This is considerably shorter than the wait time (after varying the gate voltage) for each optical measurement, during which the sample has reached the equilibrium doping densities. On the other hand, the large contact resistance has important effects on the electrical transport measurements (that is, the current flow) when electrodes are employed as source and drain. We used Pt contacts for p-doped WSe2 (ref. 32). For hole densities ≳2 × 2012 cm−2, the typical contact resistance in our devices is on the scale of hundreds of kiloohms at 1.6 K. At half-filling the measured two-point resistance is hundreds of megaohms (Fig. 2c). Given that the measured resistance is dominated by the channel resistance, we conclude from the temperature dependence of the measured resistance that the half-filling state is an insulating state. The same conclusion can be made for the full-filling state. The resistance shoots up markedly below half-filling because both the channel resistance and the contact resistance increase rapidly at low doping densities. No reliable conclusion can be made for these densities.
Determination of Zeeman splitting
The exciton Zeeman splitting was determined as the difference between the spectrally averaged exciton peak positions for the left- and righthanded reflection contrast channels. The spectrally averaged exciton peak position for each channel was obtained by a weighted sum of the photon energy, with the exciton reflection peak as the distribution function. The weighted sum was carried over a spectral range in which the exciton reflection contrast was over 90% of the peak reflection contrast. The result is insensitive to this choice down to 70% of the peak reflection contrast.
