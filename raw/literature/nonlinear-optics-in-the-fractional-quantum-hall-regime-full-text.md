Nonlinear optics in the fractional quantum Hall regime
Patrick Knüppel1,4, Sylvain ravets1,2,4*, Martin Kroner1, Stefan Fält1,3, Werner Wegscheider3 & Atac Imamoglu1*
Engineering strong interactions between optical photons is a challenge for quantum science. Polaritonics, which is based on the strong coupling of photons to atomic or electronic excitations in an optical resonator, has emerged as a promising approach to address this challenge, paving the way for applications such as photonic
gates for quantum information processing1 and photonic quantum materials for the investigation of strongly correlated driven
dissipative systems2,3. Recent experiments have demonstrated the
onset of quantum correlations in exciton-polariton systems4,5,
showing that strong polariton blockade6—the prevention of resonant injection of additional polaritons in a well delimited region by the presence of a single polariton—could be achieved if interactions were an order of magnitude stronger. Here we report time-resolved four-wave-mixing experiments on a two-dimensional
electron system embedded in an optical cavity7, demonstrating that polariton–polariton interactions are strongly enhanced when the electrons are initially in the fractional quantum Hall regime. Our experiments indicate that, in addition to strong correlations in the electronic ground state, exciton–electron interactions leading to the
formation of polaron-polaritons8–11 have a key role in enhancing the nonlinear optical response of the system. Our findings could facilitate the realization of strongly interacting photonic systems, and suggest that nonlinear optical measurements could provide information about fractional quantum Hall states that is not accessible through their linear optical response.
Polaritons are part-light part-matter quasiparticles that have recently attracted considerable interest owing to the fact that their interactions can be engineered by tuning their matter part. For example, strongly interacting Rydberg polaritons have recently been obtained using the nonlinear behaviour of Rydberg excitations in an ensemble of atoms,
which led to the demonstration of Rydberg polariton blockade12. In parallel, efforts are being made to realize polariton blockade in condensed matter systems, which holds great potential for realizing
compact and integrated synthetic quantum materials3. Exciton polaritons in semiconductor materials arise from the strong coupling of a
quantum well exciton and a cavity photon13. These photonic particles
inherit a nonlinear behaviour from exciton–exciton interactions2,13. For efficient blockade to be obtained, the polariton interaction energy,
Up, needs to be greater than the inverse lifetime γ of the polaritons6.
Recent state-of-the art experiments4,5 using photon correlation measurements in semi-integrated microcavities attained optimized values of the ratio Up/γ ≈ 0.1 in a photonic dot with an area of about
3 μm2. These experiments represent the culmination of decade-long technological developments aimed at increasing Up/γ by reducing the
photonic mode area4,5,14 and increasing the lifetime of polaritons15. Recently, several possibilities have been explored for enhancing Up through an increase of the exciton–exciton interactions U, focusing
either on biexciton Feshbach resonance16 or on excitons with a perma
nent dipole moment17–19. The experiments that we report here reveal a hitherto unexplored mechanism that underlies the nonlinearity of polaritonic excitations in a two-dimensional electron system (2DES)
in the fractional quantum Hall regime. Using time-resolved four-wavemixing experiments (see Methods), we find that polaron–polaron interactions U are enhanced by more than an order of magnitude around the fractional state at filling factor ν = 2/5 compared with other neighbouring compressible states. A comparison of U for ν = 2/5 shows an enhancement by a factor of 4.6 ± 0.9 with respect to an undoped quantum well, where the uncertainty represents one standard error of the mean. Moreover, we find that the lower polariton (LP) linewidth γ in our sample is similar to the values reported in recent experiments
demonstrating modest quantum correlations4,5, indicating that upon spatial confinement, the polariton blockade regime could be reached. The interplay between photonic excitations and a 2DES is an exciting
field20–24 with open problems, including the relation between transport
and optics25,26 and the description of exciton–electron interactions in
a magnetic field10. We study a semiconductor heterostructure that features a GaAs quantum well containing an electron system of density ne = 3
× 1010 cm−2 at the centre of an optical microcavity (see Methods). In the presence of a 2DES, electron–exciton interactions modify the excitation spectrum, and pioneering studies have shown the existence
of the strong coupling regime27,28. R. Suris29 first provided a consistent description of the new excitonic excitations that emerge in the presence
of a 2DES, which were later termed exciton polarons8–10. Strong coupling to an optical mode in turn results in the formation of polaron-polaritons as the elementary excitations of the 2DES–cavity
system9,11. Under an external magnetic field B orthogonal to the 2DES surface, discrete Landau levels form out of the conduction and heavyhole valence bands. In this Letter, we focus on resonant optical excitations associated with the highest-energy heavy-hole LL and the lowest-energy electron Landau level (LL0). As we increase B, the filling factor ν of LL0 decreases, allowing us to reach the fractional quantum Hall regime. We address transitions to the ∣↑⟩ or |↓ LL0 subbands
using σ− or σ+ circularly polarized light, respectively. We first characterize our sample using optical spectroscopy in the low-power (linear) regime. The sample is mounted inside a dilution refrigerator with fibred optical access, as shown in Fig. 1a, b. We record reflectivity spectra for several values of ν using circularly polarized light from a broadband light source. Figure 1c shows an overview of the polaron-polariton lines for our system, obtained by calculating the
difference between the spectra measured using σ− (red) and σ+ (blue) polarized light. We observe strong dispersion of the polariton energies with the magnetic field around integer and fractional values of ν. This behaviour of the linear optical spectrum stems from strong modification of the electron–exciton interactions in and around gapped quan
tum Hall states11, which in turn leads to a ν-dependent modification of the cavity–polaron coupling strength. Although the origin of this power-dependent enhancement of TLP remains unclear, this observation shows that the presence of a 2DES at finite fields has no adverse effect on γ. We speculate that the enhanced optical nonlinearity that we observe
for polaritons generated by σ− excitation is a consequence of the fragility of fractional quantum Hall states against changes in ν. Owing to the absence of electron–hole symmetry at low B fields, optical excitation of an electron to the spin-up LL0 state modifies the effective filling factor by creating quasiparticles. The size of the incompressibility gap should determine the number of extra electrons that can be excited (by photons) before the electronic system becomes compressible. Optical modification of the electronic ground state, in turn, modifies the ability of the electrons to dynamically screen the excitons, resulting in a conditional shift of the polaron-polariton resonance that underlies the nonlinear response. By contrast, for the integer quantum Hall state ν = 1, we do not observe a substantial enhancement of interactions because the electronic state is robust against small deviations in the filling factor, which in turn leads to a small modification of the photonic resonances.
Further experimental evidence supporting this tentative explanation is provided by exciting the σ+
LP resonance at ν = 2/5, where we did not
observe any enhancement of the nonlinearity. Because σ+ excitation generates an electron in the spin-down LL0 state, it does not lead to a direct modification of ν; the absence of an enhancement of nonlinearity
for σ+ excitation is therefore consistent with our explanation. We repeated the experiment around the ν = 1/3 state (B = 3.9 T), where we also did not observe enhanced nonlinearity. We argue that this is
caused by the suppression of σ− polarized polaron formation due to perfect spin polarization of the 2DES at ν ≤ 1/3, leaving the polariton
mode mostly photonic11. The large incompressibility gap of ν = 1/3 may also suppress nonlinearity. Last but not least, we measured enhanced nonlinearity also on a second sample with higher electron
density (ne = 1.4 × 1011 cm−2) for the (spin-polarized) ν = 2/3 state at B ≈ 8.5 T (see Methods). The strong enhancement of polariton–polariton interactions around fractional quantum Hall states opens up new perspectives for the study of strongly correlated electron systems as well as for photonic systems. Embedding our sample in a zero-dimensional cavity with a mode area
of A = 1 μm2 would yield U/(Aγ) = 0.85, which indicates that the polariton blockade regime is within reach. Understanding the physical mechanism of the enhanced nonlinear response and increased polariton coherence times of fractional quantum Hall states constitutes a very interesting open problem. Our experiments show that despite their qualitatively similar linear optical response, fractional and integer quantum Hall states show strikingly different nonlinear optical signatures. This suggests that nonlinear spectroscopy could reveal signatures of strongly correlated electronic systems that are not accessible by linear optical or transport measurements.

Sample structure. Our sample structure, detailed in ref. 11, features a 20-nm modulation-doped gallium arsenide (GaAs) quantum well located at the centre of a 2λ Al0.19Ga0.81As microcavity (λ, wavelength). The lower (upper) distributed Bragg reflectors consist of 25 (19) pairs of AlAs/Al0.20Ga0.80As layers. The cav
ity quality factor, measured by white-light reflectivity, is Q ≈ (5.5 ± 0.1) × 103.
The 2DES has an electron density of ne ≈ 0.33 × 1011 cm−2 and a mobility of
μ ≈ 1.6 × 106 cm2 V−1 s−1, as determined by magneto-transport measurements. We note that with these parameters, we conveniently access various integer and fractional quantum Hall states for relatively low magnetic fields, B ≤ 5 T. A thickness gradient across the sample allows us to tune the cavity energy by probing different positions on the sample. Optical characterization. We perform an initial characterization of our sample by polarization-resolved white-light reflectivity as a function of B. We couple light from a broadband light-emitting diode into an optical fibre and shine a few nanowatts of optical power onto the sample, which is placed inside a dilution refrigerator with a base temperature of 30 mK (see Fig. 1b). Monitoring the polariton spectrum around ν = 2/5 while increasing the temperature suggests that the electron temperature is lower than 200 mK. The light is focused on the sample surface using a low-NA lens (NA = 0.15) in confocal configuration. Reflected light is then collected by the same fibre and analysed in the detection path (green line in Extended Data Fig. 1). Finally, the collected light is sent to a spectrometer equipped with a nitrogen-cooled charge-coupled device. Spectra s(ω) are recorded for different values of B. field to be either right (σ+) or left (σ−) circularly polarized. Time-resolved four-wave mixing. One standard method for evaluating interactions between exciton-polaritons in two dimensions uses a resonant continuous-wave excitation laser to monitor the blue shift experienced by the LP line owing to the (Kerr-like) nonlinearity as the polariton population increases. In these experiments, however, one cannot differentiate between the contribution from fast (~10 ps) polariton–polariton interactions and other unwanted contributions due to the slow (>100 ps) buildup of an excitonic reservoir33. This issue is critical in the context of quantum Hall polaritons because the 2DES electron density is particularly sensitive to the optical power owing to possible photoionization of deep donor levels when illuminating the sample; increasing the optical power density may lead to unwanted modifications of ne and therefore to slow variations of the (ν-dependent) polariton energies, which in turn may prevent us from properly evaluating the interactions. To isolate pure polariton–polariton interactions, we use a carefully designed sample structure with reduced sensitivity of ne to light11, and perform time-resolved experiments in the pulsed-excitation regime, in which the pulse duration (~4 ps) is shorter than the polariton lifetime (>12 ps). A traditional approach to isolating the nonlinear contribution in fourwave-mixing experiments consists of introducing an angle between the two excitation beams to generate a background-free nonlinear response at a different angle34. However, the requirement for ultralow temperatures renders standard four-wavemixing experiments technically challenging to implement in our experimental geometry, which uses a fibre-coupled scanning confocal microscope in a dilution refrigerator (access to the sample is thus restricted to reflection in the collinear configuration). To separate the resonance fluorescence emitted by the sample from laser light that reflects off the surface, we use linearly polarized light for the excitation and a cross-polarization detection scheme. In this way, we suppress the background laser light by 3–4 orders of magnitude, leaving the resonance fluorescence as the dominant contribution to the detected signal. We split the picosecond laser pulse into two paths, and introduce a variable delay τ before recombining them using a beam splitter (see Extended Data Fig. 1). To distinguish the linear response from the (weaker) nonlinear response, we use an electro-optical modulator (EOM) placed between two crossed polarizers as an amplitude modulator in one arm of the interferometer. We apply a voltage with a saw-tooth profile to the EOM to create an electric field amplitude with sine modulation at frequency of ωm/2π = 8,011 Hz. We optimize the EOM input voltage profile and the EOM alignment to realize a clean sine modulation at this frequency, with less than 1% of higher-order harmonic contributions at 2ωm, 3ωm and 4ωm. In this section we present measurements on an undoped sample (ne = 0) that does not contain a 2DES but only a neutral (intrinsic) quantum well. The goal is to measure the exciton–exciton interaction strength in a standard single quantum well (thickness, 15 nm) embedded in a distributed Bragg reflector cavity and to compare it with known values of the interaction in order to establish that our measurement technique is a viable tool for measuring interactions. To obtain a single spin species and conditions comparable with those presented in the main text—namely, line

Influence of cavity detuning on the relative enhancement of U. Because polaritons interact through their matter part, a change in the polaronic content of the polaritons as a function of B would also lead to a modification of polaritonpolariton interactions: polaritons with higher polaron content will indeed show larger interactions.
